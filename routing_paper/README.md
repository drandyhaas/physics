# Whole-Route Planning of a Memory Bus Between Two BGAs

A paper, in draft for *IEEE Transactions on Computer-Aided Design of Integrated
Circuits and Systems*, on routing the bus between a processor and its DRAM
fully automatically — the densest wiring on most processor boards, and still
largely routed by hand.

📄 **[main.pdf](main.pdf)** (a draft)

The router plans every lane of a two-layer BGA-to-BGA bus completely before
any copper is laid, and carries the plan to finished, design-rule-clean copper.
On two public boards, each routed by its designer, it completes every rung of
two ladders, of up to 51 nets on the H3 (its whole bus) and 43 on the Zynq,
connected and DRC-clean, every rung's first solve proved optimal, never with
more vias than the human and always with less copper:

| board | nets | vias: router / human | copper (mm): router / human |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Allwinner H3 → DDR3 | 51 | **80** / 88 | **1262** / 1337 |
| Zynq-7020 → DDR3 | 43 | **62** / 100 | **1299** / 1577 |

On the Zynq board itself -- the board's own design rules, its planes poured, the lanes
on its two outer layers, both arrays fanned out by the toolkit's bus step -- the
router completes 45 of the bus's nets graded (Fig. 7): **70** vias to
the human's 105, **1457** mm to 1658; the production router leaves one open with
162 vias, and Freerouting two with 70.

Run on the same benches, nets and design rules and graded the same way, the
toolkit's own production router (without the bus step) leaves nets open on
four of the ten rungs, with up to 4.7 times the vias, and Freerouting
completes none (Table I, Fig. 6). On generated buses of three, four and six
routing layers every run completes, and every one with a known optimum attains
it; on two layers the short channels leave nets open.

## The idea

**The ends decide the braid.** Once each net's escape from both arrays is
fixed, the two orders of the escapes fix which pairs of nets must cross — the
inversions of a permutation. What is left free is where each crossing happens,
in what order, and where each lane changes layer. These choices interact, since
a via placed to serve one crossing serves or spoils the next, so the router
decides all of them at once:

1. **An exact constraint-programming model** (CP-SAT) chooses every crossing
   position, the crossing order and every layer change jointly, and, for the ends
   it is given, proves its plan optimal within the model. Its consistency
   rule over triples of lanes
   admits exactly the orders a wiring diagram of the permutation can realize.
2. **A linear program** places the lanes in millimeters. The constraints it
   has to break go back to the model as cuts, and an audit's findings go back as
   negotiated congestion prices, until the plan passes an audit against the
   production router's rules, or lays connected and DRC-clean.
3. **The escapes at both arrays** are chosen by an estimate of the same
   objective, so the ends themselves are not proved best. A
   differential pair is planned as one lane with a polarity at each end, and
   gets an automatic via crossover where the two ends disagree.

## The physics in it

The models read naturally as physics, and the readings were of practical use:

| routing | physics |
| --- | --- |
| a lane along the route coordinate | a worldline in one space and one time dimension |
| a crossing | a two-body scattering event |
| the triple rule on crossing order | the Yang–Baxter relation (third Reidemeister move) |
| a lane's layer | an Ising spin |
| crossing lanes must be on different layers | an antiferromagnetic bond |
| a via | a domain wall along the worldline |
| why vias are forced at all | frustration |
| the geometry LP | statics: strings at unit tension, struts between neighbors, walls at pads |
| its dual variables | contact forces, with complementary slackness as the contact law |
| an elastic constraint | a rigid-plastic strut that yields |
| a lane's layer among q > 2 | a q-state Potts spin, frustrated from q + 1 mutually crossing lanes |

The forces are also useful for reading a plan: only a small fraction of the
hard constraints carry a force, and they name the rules that hold each lane
where it is. A constraint the program could not keep is a strut that yields,
and those are what go back to the solve as cuts.

## Building

```bash
tectonic -X compile --keep-intermediates main.tex   # or pdflatex, bibtex, then pdflatex twice
python3 gen/lp_cartoon.py                           # regenerates Fig. 4 (numpy, scipy)
python3 gen/table1.py                               # Table I's rows from data/table1 and data/board
python3 gen/make_arxiv.py                           # the arXiv upload; needs the main.bbl the build kept
```

Under tectonic (XeTeX) `main.tex` switches to the OT1 font encoding, so the
local build sets its text in the same Type 1 Times as pdfLaTeX and arXiv, and
breaks it the same way. arXiv compiles with pdfLaTeX and does not run BibTeX, so
the upload carries `main.bbl`, without that switch. It publishes the source, so the script strips the comment block of
build notes and open items from the top of `main.tex`.

## Where the numbers come from

Every result is of the router repository,
[KiCadRoutingTools](https://github.com/drandyhaas/KiCadRoutingTools), at
commit **`2e319092`** (`\krtcommit` in `main.tex`). Check it out, build its
router (`python3 build_router.py`) and build the Zynq bench by its awx
README's "Building the zynq article" (from the board file in its `awx/boards/`;
the build's ladder has checkpoints 10 18 26 33 38 43 48 today, where that
section still names an older build's 47-net ladder, and the paper takes 18 to 43).
Then, with `KRT` that checkout:

```bash
# the whole route, each rung (in KRT/awx; the Zynq rungs with BASE=tmp/zynq/zynqF.kicad_pcb DEST=U2)
python3 whole_route.py K OUTDIR
# in this directory: the other three routers, then every board graded alike
python3 gen/baseline_ladders.py ladder krt OUT
python3 gen/baseline_ladders.py ladder fr OUT --jobs 3     # FREEROUTING_JAR, FREEROUTING_JAVA (2.4.1, Java 25); sets the violation charge
python3 gen/baseline_ladders.py ladder human OUT
python3 gen/baseline_ladders.py ladder ours OUT --boards "OURS/{tag}/best.kicad_pcb"
python3 gen/baseline_ladders.py render OUT DIR --tags h3_k51 --no-others    # Fig. 6: DIR/h3_k51.png is figs/k51_four.png
python3 gen/modal_synth_layers.py OUTDIR                   # the generated buses (a Modal account)
# the Zynq bus on the board itself: the planes and the bus step by the awx README's "The bus step on a real board"
# (its route_planes.py and route_bus.py commands, the bus step writing BUS.kicad_pcb), then
python3 gen/board_bus.py nets OUT --bus BUS.kicad_pcb
python3 gen/board_bus.py krt OUT
python3 gen/board_bus.py fr OUT
python3 gen/board_bus.py grade OUT --bus BUS.kicad_pcb
python3 gen/board_bus.py table OUT                          # data/board
python3 gen/table1.py OUT                                  # data/table1 and figs/table1.tex (its Full row from data/board)
python3 gen/board_bus.py render OUT figs/zynq_board_four.png
```

The paper's runs used an Apple M3 laptop (8 cores, 8 GB) with three rungs and
the board's bus step side by side, Python 3.14, OR-Tools 9.15 and SciPy 1.16; the generated buses ran on
Modal in KRT's own Linux image. Fig. 4 is computed here.
